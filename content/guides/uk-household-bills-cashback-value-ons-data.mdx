**The average UK household spends £118.40 a week — £6,156.80 a year — on housing (net), fuel and power, according to the Office for National Statistics.** That's exactly the kind of recurring direct debit spending — energy, water, council tax, broadband — that cashback current accounts are built to reward. Applying real, clearly-labelled cashback rates to that figure shows what it's actually worth, and what it isn't.

## The source figure

[ONS Family Spending in the UK, financial year ending March 2025](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/personalandhouseholdfinances/expenditure/bulletins/familyspendingintheuk/april2024tomarch2025), published 11 June 2026, puts average weekly UK household expenditure on housing (net), fuel and power at **£118.40** — the single largest category of household spend, at 18% of the £676.60 weekly total. Annualised: **£6,156.80**.

This category is a reasonable proxy for "bills you'd pay by direct debit regardless" — it's not spending you'd do to chase a reward, which is the only kind of spending worth running through a cashback calculation. It excludes groceries, fuel at the pump and discretionary card spending, which some cashback products also reward — so the figures below are a floor, not a ceiling.

## What a range of real cashback rates is worth on that

These are illustrative rates in the range UK cashback current accounts and bill-cashback schemes have advertised — not a live offer from any named provider. Net of a typical £2–£3 monthly account fee, and before any cap:

| Cashback rate | Gross annual reward on £6,156.80 | Less a £3/month fee | Net annual value |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5% | £30.78 | −£36 | **−£5.22** (fee exceeds reward) |
| 1% | £61.57 | −£36 | **£25.57** |
| 2% | £123.14 | −£36 | **£87.14** |
| 3% | £184.70 | −£36 | **£148.70** |

The table makes the actual decision visible: at 0.5%, a £3 monthly fee wipes out the reward entirely — the account would need to be fee-free, or the rate higher, to be worth holding for bills alone. Most of the real value in bill-cashback accounts sits at 1%+ once a fee applies, and many real products also apply a **monthly cap** well below the uncapped figures shown here — check the cap first, because it usually binds before the rate does. Run your own bills through the [net cashback calculator](/tools/net-cashback-calculator) with a specific account's actual rate, cap and fee.

## Why this is a floor, not an average person's total

Two things this figure deliberately doesn't do:

- **It's not "the average household's cashback."** No public data tracks actual UK cashback uptake or redemption, so this isn't an estimate of what households currently earn — it's what the spending they already have would return under stated rates, which is a fair test of whether a specific account is worth opening.
- **It only covers one ONS category.** Households also spend on food, transport fuel and communications — some of which qualifies for cashback on other products (supermarket cashback cards, fuel cards). Adding those would raise the eligible base above £6,156.80, not lower it.

## The comparison that actually matters

The number that matters for any individual household isn't the ONS average — it's your own recurring direct debits against the specific account's fee, cap and rate.
